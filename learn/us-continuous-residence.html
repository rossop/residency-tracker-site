US naturalization: continuous residence and physical presence

To naturalize as a US citizen you generally need 5 years of continuous residence as a permanent resident (3 years for some spouses of US citizens) and physical presence in the US for at least half of that time, which is 30 months (913 days) in the general 5-year case. An absence longer than 6 months but under a year is presumed to interrupt continuous residence, while one lasting a year or longer breaks it unless you hold an approved Form N-470.

Quick answers

How is physical presence counted?

USCIS treats both your departure day and your return day as days present in the US.

What does an absence over six months do?

An absence longer than 6 months but shorter than 1 year is presumed to break continuity. You can rebut the presumption with evidence, such as keeping US employment, having immediate family remain in the US, or retaining your US home.

What does an absence of a year or more do?

It breaks continuity of residence, unless you have an approved Application to Preserve Residence for Naturalization Purposes (Form N-470).

Is continuous residence the same as physical presence?

No. They are separate requirements and you must satisfy both. A short trip does not break continuous residence but is still days outside the US for the physical presence total.

Does a green card prove residence?

Not by itself. USCIS looks at where you actually maintain your dwelling and your presence, supported by documents.

Worked examples

Counting a trip. You leave on 10 June and return on 20 June. Both 10 June and 20 June count as days present, so the days outside the US are 11 to 19 June, which is 9 days.

Long absence. You spend seven months abroad. That is more than 6 months and less than a year, so USCIS presumes it broke continuous residence, and you would need evidence to rebut it. The seven months also reduce your physical presence total, which must still reach 913 days over the 5-year period.
