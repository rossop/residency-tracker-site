What is a 183-day rule?

There is no single worldwide 183-day rule. 183 days, about half a year, is a threshold that appears in different places with different meanings: in tax treaties following Article 15 of the OECD Model Tax Convention, where short work stays are taxed only in the home country if you stay no more than 183 days and other conditions are met, and in national residence tests such as the UK's and the US's. The period, the way days are counted and the consequence all differ, so check the rule that applies to you.

Quick answers

What does the OECD Model say?

Article 15(2) lets the state where you are working not tax your employment income if your stays there total 183 days or fewer within any 12-month span that starts or finishes in the relevant fiscal year, your pay comes from an employer who is not a resident of that state, and the pay is not borne by a permanent establishment there. All conditions must be met, and each treaty has its own wording.

Is 183 days a residency test in the UK?

Partly. Spending 183 or more days in the UK within one tax year, which runs from 6 April to 5 April, makes you UK resident under the first automatic UK test. A day counts when midnight finds you in the UK.

Is it the same in the US?

No. The US substantial presence test needs a weighted total of 183 days, and also a minimum of 31 days in the current year. The total adds all days in the current year, a third of the prior year's days and a sixth of those from the year before that. Any day in the US at any time counts.

Do I get taxed nowhere if I stay under 183 days?

No. Staying under a threshold does not mean you are not resident or taxable somewhere. Other tests, such as home, ties or domicile, can make you resident, and the treaty rule only decides which country may tax one kind of income.

How do countries count days?

It varies: midnight presence, any part of a day, or full days. The OECD Commentary on Article 15 discusses counting days of physical presence. Read the rule and treaty that apply to you.

Worked example

A resident of country A works in country B for 100 days in a calendar year, paid by an employer in country A that has no permanent establishment in B. Under a treaty based on the OECD Model, 100 is under 183 and the other two conditions are met, so B may not tax that pay.

If the stay were 200 days, the first condition fails and B may tax the income earned there. The exact periods, day counting and treaty wording decide the outcome in practice, so take advice.

Different countries also run their own tests: see UK day counting and the US substantial presence test.
